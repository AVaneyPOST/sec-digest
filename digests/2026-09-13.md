## Telecom

**ST Engineering iDirect Evolution iQ-Series / 3315-Series / 9-Series VSAT terminals — CVE-2026-38056 (CVSS 9.4) + CVE-2026-38058 + CVE-2026-38059 — published 2026-09-12. HIGH/CRITICAL for satcom estate.** Three chained vulnerabilities in one of the two dominant enterprise VSAT product families:
- **CVE-2026-38059 — unauthenticated identity disclosure.** The `/api/identity` and root `/api/` REST endpoints on iQ200 respond without authentication, returning the terminal's serial number, Device ID (DID), Terminal Private Key identifier (TPK), MAC and exact firmware string. A remote scanner reaches all of it before any credential is asked for.
- **CVE-2026-38058 — password-hash exposure.** The device configuration endpoint returns the full JSON blob including a `SECURITY` section carrying MD5-crypt hashes for the root SSH account and the web admin. An authenticated web user can pull the hashes and crack offline; combine with 38059 to identify a target modem first and 38056 or a legit tech account to log in.
- **CVE-2026-38056 (CVSS 9.4) — local privilege escalation.** A pre-configured low-privilege field-technician account on iQ200 firmware 23.0.1.0 can escalate to full admin without additional authentication. Since the low-privilege credentials are well-known to field techs and often documented in operator runbooks, "local console access" is a routine constraint, not a hard gate.

Why it matters to a telco / satcom offensive-security practitioner: iDirect iQ200 sits at the network edge of a lot of satcom deployments where the modem is the single link to the outside — maritime vessels, offshore rigs, MPO/oil-gas remote sites, disaster-response comms and defense forward-operating deployments. A compromised iQ200 can be used to tamper with telemetry, disrupt the site's only communications link, sniff the LAN behind it, or pivot from the site into the operator's private network. The 38059/38058 combo is remotely usable against any exposed management interface (SDW/WAN-side, or LAN-side after any beach-head); 38056 is the "field tech laptop" foothold. Fixed firmware: 4.5.3.0. Sources: [OpenCVE CVE-2026-38056](https://app.opencve.io/cve/CVE-2026-38056), [VulDB CVE-2026-38059](https://vuldb.com/vuln/377485), [OpenCVE CVE-2026-38058](https://app.opencve.io/cve/CVE-2026-38058), [Threat Radar 38056](https://radar.offseq.com/threat/cve-2026-38056-cwe-862-in-st-engineering-idirect-evolution-iqseries-terminals-1453bbe9b5508b13), [Threat Radar 38058](https://radar.offseq.com/threat/cve-2026-38058-cwe-497-in-st-engineering-idirect-evolution-iqseries-terminals-e72edb5b0a465055), [ThreatINT 38056](https://cve.threatint.com/CVE/CVE-2026-38056), [ThreatINT 38058](https://cve.threatint.com/CVE/CVE-2026-38058). ## General high-severity

**Revolut — customer data breach disclosed 2026-09-12.** UK-headquartered fintech Revolut confirmed to TechCrunch and Bloomberg on 2026-09-12 that it had disclosed sensitive customer information to an unauthorized third party after receiving fraudulent legal-process requests sent from what appeared to be a legitimate government-agency email domain (Revolut has not named the impersonated agency). The spoof passed standard email-authentication (SPF/DKIM/DMARC) checks, and Revolut's compliance/KYC-response team then sent back customer data as if it were responding to a genuine subpoena or MLAT-adjacent request. Data disclosed to the attacker included: full name, date of birth, postal and email addresses, phone numbers, identity documents (passports, driver's licenses), verification selfies, account statements, IBANs, withdrawal histories, and — the reason this went straight to crypto press — Bitcoin transaction activity for affected customers. Revolut says a "limited" number of customers were affected but has not published a count; systems and funds are unaffected; the sender address was blocked and the relevant agency, law enforcement and regulators were notified. Practitioner take (telecom-adjacent): a customer packet consisting of passport scan + verification selfie + full contact set + on-chain wallet history is precisely the input that makes carrier-CSR / eSIM-port social engineering work against a specific target — an attacker no longer needs to guess who's worth SIM-swapping. Expect this data (or its extract) to end up sold or given to SIM-swap crews and used against Revolut users' phone numbers on carriers with weak port verification. Also expect similar-provenance fake-legal-process attacks against telcos' own law-enforcement request channels — the vector is not fintech-specific. Sources: [TechCrunch (in window)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/12/revolut-confirms-customer-data-breach-through-fake-government-requests/), [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-12/revolut-says-some-customer-data-were-exposed-in-email-based-scam), [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/378114/revolut-passports-bitcoin-activity-data-breach), [CryptoTimes](https://www.cryptotimes.io/2026/09/12/revolut-handed-over-bitcoin-histories-passports-on-spoofed-government-email/), [Northeast Times](https://northeasttimes.com/2026/09/12/revolut-gave-away-passport-and-bitcoin-records-after-spoofed-agency-email-passed/), [FraudBeat](https://www.fraudbeat.com/revolut-security-breach/), [Parameter](https://parameter.io/revolut-data-breach-fraudulent-government-email-leads-to-bitcoin-record-leak/).
